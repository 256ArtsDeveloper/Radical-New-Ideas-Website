Theme Parks Don't Need Lines

A day at Disneyland means five and a half hours standing in line. None of it is necessary.

Chapter 1

Half your day is a line

Take eight of Disneyland's most popular rides and add up their average waits.1

That's five and a half hours standing in line for about forty minutes on rides.

Nearly half your day goes to waiting.

Waits are all-time averages from Queue-Times. Ride time assumes about five minutes each.

Disneyland's Rise of the Resistance is the worst of them. It averages a 64-minute wait.1

On a busy afternoon it peaks above 90 minutes.2

Ask guests what the worst part of a theme park is.

United States

Half of Americans and 70% of Brits give the same answer: the lines.3

It has been this way from the start. When Disneyland opened in 1955, the line opened with it.

Since then the rides have gone from teacups to trackless robots that sync with projection domes.

The line is the one thing that hasn't changed. You stand. You shuffle. You hope.

Chapter 2

The line does nothing

Riders per hour1,700
Standing in line—

A line doesn't make room.

Rise of the Resistance seats about 1,700 riders an hour at best.4 Over a 12-hour day that's about 20,000 rides. The line outside doesn't add a single seat.

Disneyland gets about 47,000 guests on an average day.5 Fewer than half of them can ride, however long the line gets.

So the line isn't there to move people. It's there to choose them: whoever is willing to stand longest.

A schedule can make the same choice.

The ride stays just as full, and nobody has to stand anywhere.

What if everyone wants the same ride? Then not everyone gets it, exactly as today.

The difference is that you find out in the morning, not after 64 minutes on your feet. And the park can share the seats fairly: everyone's first choice before anyone's second.

Parks have come close for decades. Disney handed out free FastPasses in 1999. In 2017 a water park in Orlando opened with no physical lines at all.6

Then, one by one, the free versions disappeared.7,8

FastPass was bolted onto the line.

FastPass riders took about four of every five seats, so the regular line beside it crawled.9 Most guests still stood in it.

Volcano Bay hid the line instead of ending it.

You could hold a place in only one virtual line at a time, and waits reached three hours.10 Universal dropped the system in 2025 without giving a reason.

Neither one planned your day.

A virtual line is still a line: first come, first served, one ride at a time. A schedule looks at every guest and every ride at once.

The receipt doesn't change.

Today a park sells you a $100 ticket, then four $20 fast passes during the day. They squeeze in four extra rides, but you still stand in line for the other eight.

Instead, sell the same $100 ticket with reservations included, and the same four $20 add-ons as extra ride reservations. You can still buy them on impulse from your phone, mid-afternoon.

The price is the same. The upsell is the same. The five and a half hours in line are gone.

And the guest who never buys an add-on? Today they get the worst day in the park.

Under this model they get the same eight rides, and they don't wait for any of them.

Lines cost parks money too.

75% of US theme park guests say they sometimes skip food because of lines, and 65% skip merchandise.12

A guest in line can't buy a churro.

Give them their time back and they'll spend it in the park.

Tell the park what you want to ride.

You pick rides, not times. Rank them that morning, or weeks before.

It works out when.

The park knows every ride's capacity and every guest's list. It hands you a day with a seat on each ride, the way a maps app hands you a route.

Running late? The plan moves.

Today's paid passes turn one person in every family into the "designated scroller," rebooking rides on a phone all day.13 This is the opposite. When lunch runs long or a ride breaks down, your seat is rebooked for you.

Everything between rides is unplanned.

Wander, eat, play. Go when your phone buzzes.

Chapter 6

Give the line somewhere to go

A line is also where a park keeps people.

A 64-minute line for a ride that seats 1,700 an hour is about 1,800 people parked in one building.

Take the lines away and all of them are back in the park, each with five and a half hours to fill.

So the real job is building places worth those hours.

Cruise ships already run this way.

Parks already build beautiful places. They just put them in the line.

Universal's Harry Potter and the Forbidden Journey has one of the best queues ever built: a walk through Hogwarts castle, past moving portraits, Dumbledore's office and the Gryffindor common room.15

Guests only ever see it in one of two ways.

When the line is long, it's a crowded waiting room.

When the line is short, you rush through.

Now picture the same castle as a walk-through you choose to visit, at your own pace.

That's an attraction in its own right. The money is already being spent. Spend it on places guests choose to be.

Meow Wolf

A walk-through art experience with no ride system at all. Guests stay for a median of two hours, and the Las Vegas location drew a million visitors in its first year.16

Pirate's Lair and Redwood Creek

Disneyland's own rope bridges, caves and climbing trails. Kids spend hours there, and nobody has to queue.

Seven Dwarfs Mine Train

The queue hides touchscreen games inside the ride building. Families play while they wait instead of staring at the back of a stranger's head.

Super Nintendo World

The whole land is a game. Guests jump to hit blocks and collect keys between rides, so the time between rides counts too.

None of this is new.

Parks already know how to build places where guests happily spend hours. There just aren't enough of them.

Reach outside the toolbox

Theme parks keep reaching for the same two tools: the ride and the show. Other businesses already hold people happily for hours with far simpler ones.

Each costs far less than a ride and has no hard capacity limit. Theme one as lavishly as a ride and it stops being a bowling alley. It becomes a reason to visit.

Better still, build the play zone into the ride building itself. Mine Train does a small version of this. Go all the way.

Keep playing until your phone says it's your turn. The ride is a thirty-second walk away.

Theme parks have spent seventy years perfecting the line.

The next seventy should be spent designing the day.

Move every guest to the reservation system.

Sell more rides, not shorter lines.

Spend the queue budget on places to play and rest.

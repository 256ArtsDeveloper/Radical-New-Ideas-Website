Pressure

Life builds pressure.

It starts with money.

Then work.

Then the bigger things: how fairly we're treated, and the world we're leaving behind.

Frustration is pressure, and pressure means something is broken.

A healthy society lets it out, listens, and fixes the cause.

Every pressure cooker has a valve.

Heat builds pressure inside.

The valve lets a little out, all the time, so the pot never explodes.

So we built valves. Lots of them.

Every one does the same job: turn frustration into something you can do.

And some unofficial ones.

When the front-door valves are hard to use, people make their own valves to let the pressure out.

Deadlines, forms, gatekeepers. Most people never learn them all, so some pressure never gets out.

The smallest things in your life are the easiest to leave. You can cancel a streaming app in a few minutes, and switch banks in a week.

The biggest things get the stiffest valves.

Your laws, taxes and police take years to change, or a visa to escape.

Big bundles are rigid.

When everything comes in one package, nothing can change without changing it all. So it falls behind what people need.

Switching times are rough, illustrative estimates.

Switching only works if there's somewhere to switch to.

Chapter 3

Plugged valves

Authoritarians

plug political valves

  • Cancel elections, or fake them.
  • Jail critics and ban protests.
  • Close the borders.
Monopolists

plug market valves

  • Buy up every competitor.
  • Lock you into their ecosystem.
  • Make cancelling a maze.

Some people want the valves plugged.

Authoritarians plug the political ones.

Monopolists plug the market ones.

Why? Because trapped pressure is controlled pressure.

If you can't vote them out, can't complain and can't leave, your unhappiness costs them nothing. They don't have to get better. They just have to keep the lid on.

But a plug doesn't remove pressure.

It stores it.

Eventually, something gives.

Plugged: no free elections, no free market, no leaving.

Gave: decades of black markets, then the whole union dissolved in months.3

Plugged: the Treaty of Versailles loaded Germany with war debt it had no say in.

Gave: hyperinflation and depression. Voters turned to Hitler, who promised to tear it up. Then World War II.4

Plugged: taxed by a Parliament they had no seats in.

Gave: petitions were ignored, so the colonies fought and left. Britain lost them for good.5

It happens to companies too.

Plugged: one provider per street, and 200 channels to get the 5 you wanted.

Gave: once streaming arrived, tens of millions of homes left.6

Plugged: cities capped licences, so service never had to improve.

Gave: Uber arrived. A New York medallion fell from $1.3M to under $200K.7

Plugged: $18 CDs to get the one song you wanted, and lawsuits against fans.

Gave: Napster and mass piracy. US music sales fell by more than half.8

Countries and companies alike.

Plug the valve, and the pressure finds another way out.

Open valves: steady, survivablePlugged valves: calm… then collapse

Little releases, or one big blast.

Open valves let pressure out a little at a time. It never gets near the breaking point.

Plugged valves look calm for years. Then they collapse.

Valves aren't a weakness.

They're what keeps the whole thing standing. A society that lets pressure out is more stable, not less.

Chapter 4

A valve that always works

What if one valve worked the same way everywhere?

Not instead of the others. Underneath them. One valve every group must have, whatever else it offers, that works the same for a country, a company or a club.

Leave.

Don't like it? Go somewhere better.
No permission. No majority. No waiting.

Optional · each group chooses
Elections
Member councils
Reviews

Guaranteed · everywhere
Leaving

Leaving is the floor. Every group has to offer it, and it works the same way in all of them.

Groups add their own internal valves on top.

A group can run itself like a democracy, a co-op, a monarchy or a company. Only the floor is fixed.

Members can leave
Member
The service has gotten worse.
Leadership
Thank you for your feedback.
Sorry. We'll fix it this month. Please don't go.

Leaving makes the other valves work.

It's always on: it works today, not at the next election. And it's personal: you don't have to convince millions of people.

When members can't leave, a complaint is easy to ignore.

When they can, leaders listen. Leaving gives voice teeth.

Economist Albert Hirschman called these "voice" and "exit" in 1970. Exit is what makes leaders take voice seriously.1

One government, one package

So why can't we just leave our government?

Because leaving a country means leaving everything at once: your home, your job, your friends, your language.

Government is one giant bundle. Too heavy to walk away from.

Break the bundle.

Make each one a separate service, the way your bank, phone plan and streaming apps already are.

Small pieces are easy to leave.

Switching banks doesn't mean moving house. Neither should switching your kid's school.

When leaving is easy, pressure comes out a little at a time, all the time. No blast.

Chapter 5

A Modular Society

An old idea

Choose your government the way you choose an insurer, without moving.

First proposed by Belgian writer Paul Émile de Puydt in 1860. He called it panarchy.2

Progressive: taxes pay for healthcareConservative: low taxes, no healthcare

Picture four neighbours on one street.

Alex and Riley join a progressive government. Its taxes pay for their healthcare with provider A.

Riley wants more, so tops up with provider B as well.

Sam and Jordan join a conservative government: low taxes, no healthcare.

Sam buys healthcare from provider A directly. Jordan picks B.

Same street. Four different setups. Nobody had to move.

3Modularity

Every group is just an organization.

Government, company, church or co-op. None gets powers the others can't have.

You belong because you joined.

Not because of where you happened to be born.

Services come apart.

One organization can sell firefighting or courts to members of many others.

Top-down Member-run Height = power

Today, power is a pyramid.

One government sits in the middle. Big companies cluster around it, almost as tall and just as steep. Smaller companies crowd around them.

And it stacks.

Where the bases overlap, the same people answer to a government and the companies they can't do without. Pressure from both, valves for neither.

A modular society is a range of hills.

Power spreads across many groups. Some are run from the top. Some are run by their members. Most land somewhere in between. Some are still big.

They overlap too, but every overlap is one you chose. None grows taller than its members let it.

The only rule

Anyone may leave. No one may be held.

Everything else is a deal between people who chose to be there. One tiny rule is hard to twist, capture or bury in exceptions.

Charging ✓Holding ✗

Leaving can cost you.

Like breaking a phone contract early: you might owe a fee, and they can collect it like any debt.

It can't be stopped.

Nobody can lock the door. Not with walls, not with guards, not with threats.

Good organizations will even pay your exit fee to win you over, just like phone carriers do today.

Long promises still work.

Want a safety net? Join a caring organization while you're young, before you know if you'll be rich, and agree to pay in by income.

If you later leave for a low-tax group, the debt comes with you.

Fairness becomes something you can sign up for.

Who protects the right to leave?

Asylum

Others take in anyone escaping, and won't send them back.

Refusal to deal

No trade, no currency, no land recognition for groups that trap people.

Exit guarantors

Organizations that sell escape as a service. Joining one signals "you can always leave."

Coalitions

As a last resort, everyone acts together against a group that holds its members.

No world government needed. It works the way countries enforce treaties today.

It takes no side.

Progressive

High taxes, universal healthcare, strong safety net.

Conservative

Traditional values, low taxes, local control.

Commune

Shared land, shared work, shared everything.

Each can build exactly the society it wants. None can force you to join.

It would probably look… familiar.

Most people would join something like a government today: security, courts and land, in one package. Many would still hold elections inside.

The difference? You chose it. And you could choose another.

The hard questions

War?

Fighting is expensive.

Most disputes would be negotiated, the way insurers settle claims. But history says this one needs proof.

A new giant?

Big is fine. Trapping isn't.

The danger is a group strong enough to end the right to leave. Medieval Iceland shows it can happen.9

Shared air & oceans?

The weakest point.

Nobody owns the climate. It depends on treaties between organizations. Still an open question.

Pressure always finds a way out.

The only question is whether we build the valve, or wait for the blast.

Make leaving the one rule every group must follow.

Break government into services you can switch.

Let people choose, without moving.

What do you call this?

A modular society. Older names each cover part of it. None has the one rule: anyone may leave, no one may be held.

de Puydt, 1860

Panarchy

Fits: Choose your government without moving.

Differs: Governments stay governments: no unbundling, no exit rule. Sounds like “anarchy,” and “pan-” suggests world rule.

Scott Alexander; Kukathas

Archipelago

Fits: The right to leave as the one guarantee, children included.

Differs: Islands imply territory. Here, law follows people, and services don’t come apart.

Friedman; Ostrom

Polycentric law

Fits: Competing courts and police, with no monopoly.

Differs: Describes the outcome, not the exit rule. Academic.

Frey & Eichenberger

Competing jurisdictions

Fits: Each service its own jurisdiction, overlapping and competing.

Differs: Still governments only. Companies and co-ops don’t count, and there’s no exit rule.

Rothbard; Friedman

Market anarchism

Fits: No group has powers the others lack, so no state.

Differs: Picks a side: markets. This welcomes communes and redistribution too.

Hirschman, 1970

Exit

Fits: Names the one rule directly.

Differs: Tied to tech-right projects that are territorial or founder-run.

19th century

Voluntaryism

Fits: You belong because you joined.

Differs: Associated with “taxation is theft.” Here, taxes and debts you signed up for still bind.

Renner & Bauer, ~1900

Personal federalism

Fits: Law follows people, not land.

Differs: Built for ethnic minorities inside one state. Dry.

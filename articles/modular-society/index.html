Radical New Ideas

Switch governments without moving

Your schools, police and laws come as one bundle you can't leave. Break it into services you choose, the way you choose a bank or a phone plan.

Chapter 1

Stuck

Pressure

You can't switch the things that matter most.

Your kid's school comes with your address. In America's big cities, a home near a top-scoring public school costs 2.4 times as much as one near a low-scoring school.1

The permit office is the only one there is, so it never has to hurry.

And your government comes with your birthplace. In 2024, 75 million Americans voted for the side that lost.2 They got the winner's government anyway.

Leaving is rarely a real option. Fewer than 4 in 100 people live outside the country they were born in.3

Frustration is pressure.

And when you can't leave, nobody has to listen. In October 2026, 14% of Americans approved of Congress. Yet in every election since 1964, at least 85% of House members who ran again have won.4

Every pressure cooker has a valve.

Heat builds pressure inside.

The valve lets a little out, all the time, so the pot never explodes.

Societies have valves too.

Voting, protesting and striking let you push back. Quitting, cancelling and switching let you walk away.

Two ways to let pressure out.

Push back, or walk away. Block both and the pressure doesn't vanish. It leaks.

Valve 1

Courts, police, schools and roads, all in one package

The small things in your life are easy to leave. Cancel a streaming app in minutes. Move your phone number to a new carrier in one business day.5 In Britain, switch banks in seven working days, guaranteed.6

The biggest things get the stiffest valves.

Zoom out far enough to fit them on the same chart, and the small things all but vanish.

Your laws, taxes and police change at the next election, up to four years away. Or you leave the country: get a visa, then live there five years to become a US citizen.7

Big bundles are rigid.

Courts, police, schools and roads come as one package. You can't leave one without leaving them all.

That's the whole argument. You can't leave your government because it's a bundle. Break the bundle, and you can.

Chapter 2

Plugged valves

Authoritarians

plug political valves

  • Cancel elections, or fake them.
  • Jail critics and ban protests.
  • Close the borders.
Monopolists

plug market valves

  • Buy up every competitor.
  • Lock you into their ecosystem.
  • Make cancelling a maze.

Some people want the valves plugged.

Authoritarians plug the political ones.

Monopolists plug the market ones.

Why? Because trapped pressure is controlled pressure.

If you can't vote them out, can't complain and can't leave, your unhappiness costs them nothing. They don't have to get better. They just have to keep the lid on.

But a plug doesn't remove pressure.

It stores it.

Eventually, something gives.

Plugged: no free elections, no free market, no leaving.

Gave: decades of black markets, then the whole union dissolved in months.8

Plugged: taxed by a Parliament they had no seats in.

Gave: petitions were ignored, so the colonies fought and left. Britain lost them for good.9

It happens to companies too.

Plugged: one provider per street, and 200 channels to get the 5 you wanted.

Gave: once streaming arrived, tens of millions of homes left.10

Plugged: $18 CDs to get the one song you wanted, and lawsuits against fans.

Gave: Napster and mass piracy. US music sales fell by more than half.11

Countries and companies alike.

Plug the valve, and the pressure finds another way out.

Open valves: steady, survivablePlugged valves: calm… then collapse

Little releases, or one big blast.

Open valves let pressure out a little at a time. It never gets near the breaking point.

Plugged valves look calm for years. Then they collapse.

Americans who trust the federal government to do what is right always or most of the time. Pew Research Center.

Nobody plugged our valves. They're just stiff.

One vote, every few years, for the whole bundle at once. In between, pressure builds with nowhere to go.

In 1964, 77% of Americans trusted Washington to do the right thing most of the time. By 2025, 17% did.12

That's why every election feels like an emergency.

The winner takes the whole bundle. Lose, and every rule you care about sits in someone else's hands for years.

Valves aren't a weakness.

They're what keeps the whole thing standing. A society that lets pressure out is more stable, not less.

Chapter 3

A valve that always works

What if one valve worked the same way everywhere?

Not instead of the others. Underneath them. One valve every group must have, whatever else it offers, that works the same for a country, a company or a club.

Leave.

Don't like it? Go somewhere better.
No permission. No majority. No waiting.

Leaving

Leaving is the floor. Every group has to offer it, and it works the same way in all of them.

Groups add their own internal valves on top.

A group can run itself like a democracy, a co-op, a monarchy or a company. Only the floor is fixed.

Members can leave
Member
The service has gotten worse.
Leadership
Thank you for your feedback.
Sorry. We'll fix it this month. Please don't go.

Leaving makes the other valves work.

It's always on: it works today, not at the next election. And it's personal: you don't have to convince millions of people.

When members can't leave, a complaint is easy to ignore.

When they can, leaders listen. Leaving gives voice teeth.

Economist Albert Hirschman called these "voice" and "exit" in 1970. Exit is what makes leaders take voice seriously.13

One government, one package

So why can't we just leave our government?

Because leaving a country means leaving everything at once: your home, your job, your friends, your language.

Government is one giant bundle. Too heavy to walk away from.

Break the bundle.

Make each one a separate service, the way your bank, phone plan and streaming apps already are.

Small pieces are easy to leave.

Switching banks doesn't mean moving house. Neither should switching your kid's school.

When leaving is easy, pressure comes out a little at a time, all the time. No blast.

Chapter 4

A Modular Society

An old idea

Choose your government the way you choose an insurer, without moving.

First proposed by Belgian writer Paul Émile de Puydt in 1860. He called it panarchy.14 Break the bundle into pieces too, and you get a modular society.

Progressive: higher taxes, school includedConservative: low taxes, buy your ownGovernment paysFamily pays

Picture four neighbours on one street.

Alex and Riley join a progressive government. Higher taxes, and it pays for their kids' school.

Sam and Jordan join a conservative one: low taxes, and they pay for school themselves.

Sam picks School A. Jordan picks School B.

Then School A gets worse.

Today, Riley would have to move house or wait for an election.

Here, Riley moves the kids to School B this month. Same house. Same government, which now pays School B instead.

School A feels it at once. Riley's money is gone, and Alex and Sam are watching.

It fixes the problem, or it shrinks. That's pressure coming out a little at a time.

Same street. Four setups. Nobody moved.

You belong because you joined, not because of where you were born.

What happens when neighbours clash?

Sam's bike goes missing. It turns up in Alex's garage. They pay for different police and different courts.

Their providers saw this coming. They deal with each other every day, so they signed the rules in advance: who investigates, which court hears it, what the penalty is.

You already live with this.

When two drivers crash, their insurers settle it between themselves, under rules agreed long before.15 Most claims never reach a courtroom.

And a court that refuses to sign with the others? Its members can't settle anything with anyone. They leave for one that can.

Pieces of this already work.

93

public health funds compete in Germany

75 million people pick one, and can switch without moving.16

7

working days to switch banks in Britain

Guaranteed, with your payments moved for you. Over 11 million switches since 2013.6

172

countries enforce private courts

Two companies can name their own arbitrator in a contract. Courts in 172 countries will uphold the ruling.17

332

years Iceland ran on switchable chieftains

From 930, a farmer chose his chieftain, and could switch to another without moving farm.18

None of these is a whole modular society. Each is one piece of government people already switch.

The only rule

Anyone may leave. No one may be held.

It binds governments, companies, churches and co-ops alike. None gets powers the others can't have. Everything else is a deal between people who chose to be there. One tiny rule is hard to twist, capture or bury in exceptions.

Charging ✓Holding ✗

Leaving can cost you.

Only what you signed up for: unpaid bills, or an exit fee you agreed to when you joined. Like breaking a phone contract early.

It can't be stopped.

You walk out first and settle up after. Nobody can lock the door until you pay. Not with walls, not with guards, not with threats.

A fee can't become a wall.

Your new group's court collects it, under the same advance agreements that settled Sam's bike. A fee too big for any outside court to enforce is just a wall, and walls break the rule.

Good organizations will even pay your exit fee to win you over, just like phone carriers do today.

Won't the rich just walk away from the bill?

Not if they signed. A safety net is a long promise. You join a caring organization while you're young, before you know if you'll be rich, and agree to pay in by income.

If you later leave for a low-tax group, the debt comes with you. Your new group's court collects it, like any other contract.

Fairness becomes something you can sign up for.

Who protects the right to leave?

Asylum

Others take in anyone escaping, and won't send them back.

Refusal to deal

No trade, no currency, no land recognition for groups that trap people.

Exit guarantors

Organizations that sell escape as a service. Joining one signals "you can always leave."

Coalitions

As a last resort, everyone acts together against a group that holds its members.

No world government needed. It works the way countries enforce treaties today.

It takes no side.

Progressive

High taxes, universal healthcare, strong safety net.

Conservative

Traditional values, low taxes, local control.

Commune

Shared land, shared work, shared everything.

Each can build exactly the society it wants. None can force you to join.

It would probably look… familiar.

Most people would pick a ready-made package, something like a government today: security, courts and land in one plan. Many would still hold elections inside.

The difference is the switch on every line.

You chose it. And you could choose another.

Most people would rarely touch it. They wouldn't need to. A provider that can lose you tomorrow behaves today.

The hard questions

War?

Fighting is expensive.

A group that starts a war pays for it, and its members can walk out. So most disputes get settled like insurance claims. That's no guarantee. Countries don't offer one either.

A new giant?

Big is fine. Trapping isn't.

A giant that locks its doors faces everyone else at once: asylum, boycott, coalition. Iceland's version held for 332 years before a few chieftains grew strong enough to end it.18

Shared air & oceans?

Treaties, like today.

Nobody owns the climate, here or now. Both systems depend on agreements between groups. It's the weakest point, and it's no weaker than what we have.

Roads & pipes?

Some things stay local.

Streets and sewers are tied to the ground. Neighbours run them together, the way condo boards and utilities do now, apart from everything else. Schools, healthcare, courts and laws aren't tied to the ground. Those follow you.

Pressure always finds a way out.

The only question is whether we build the valve, or wait for the blast.

What do you call this?

A modular society. Older names each cover part of it. None has the one rule: anyone may leave, no one may be held.

de Puydt, 1860

Panarchy

Fits: Choose your government without moving.

Differs: Governments stay governments: no unbundling, no exit rule. Sounds like “anarchy,” and “pan-” suggests world rule.

Scott Alexander; Kukathas

Archipelago

Fits: The right to leave as the one guarantee, children included.

Differs: Islands imply territory. Here, law follows people, and services don’t come apart.

Friedman; Ostrom

Polycentric law

Fits: Competing courts and police, with no monopoly.

Differs: Describes the outcome, not the exit rule. Academic.

Frey & Eichenberger

Competing jurisdictions

Fits: Each service its own jurisdiction, overlapping and competing.

Differs: Still governments only. Companies and co-ops don’t count, and there’s no exit rule.

Rothbard; Friedman

Market anarchism

Fits: No group has powers the others lack, so no state.

Differs: Picks a side: markets. This welcomes communes and redistribution too.

Hirschman, 1970

Exit

Fits: Names the one rule directly.

Differs: Tied to tech-right projects that are territorial or founder-run.

19th century

Voluntaryism

Fits: You belong because you joined.

Differs: Associated with “taxation is theft.” Here, taxes and debts you signed up for still bind.

Renner & Bauer, ~1900

Personal federalism

Fits: Law follows people, not land.

Differs: Built for ethnic minorities inside one state. Dry.

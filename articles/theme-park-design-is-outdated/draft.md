# Theme Park Design Is Outdated — Scrollytelling Draft

This draft is written for a scroll-driven webpage. Each **beat** is one screen: a short block of copy paired with a graphic that animates as the reader scrolls. Copy blocks are kept to roughly 60 words or fewer. The **Visual** and **On scroll** lines are build notes, not reader-facing text.

**Recurring devices** (introduce each one once, then reuse it):
- **The Day Bar:** a horizontal 12-hour timeline of one guest's day. It first appears full of gray "waiting" blocks and comes back at the end full of color.
- **The Cost/Enjoyment chart:** a 2×2 grid that plots each thing a park builds by cost and by how much guests enjoy it.
- **Dot guests:** each dot is a guest. They flow, queue, scatter and regroup to show capacity ideas without text.

## Opening

### Beat 0 — Title
**Visual:** a full-bleed overhead illustration of a switchback queue packed with dot guests, under a hot, washed-out sky. A wait-time sign reads `75 MIN`.
**On scroll:** the camera pulls up and out. The queue keeps going, filling the screen.

**Copy**
# Theme Park Design Is Outdated
We've spent seventy years perfecting the line. It's time to get rid of it.

### Beat 1 — Since the beginning
**Visual:** a vintage-style illustration of a 1955 park entrance, with a line already forming.
**On scroll:** it cross-fades to the same view today. The line is longer.

**Copy**
When Disneyland opened in 1955, the line opened with it. Since then the rides have gone from teacups to trackless robots that sync with projection domes. The line is the one thing that hasn't changed. You stand. You shuffle. You hope.

### Beat 2 — The Day Bar
**Visual:** the Day Bar runs from 9 AM to 9 PM.
**On scroll:** eight gray blocks fill in one by one, each labeled with a ride. Thin colored slivers show the time actually spent riding. A counter adds up the totals: **6h 00m waiting** and **~0h 40m riding**.

**Copy**
Take one ordinary day at Disneyland: eight popular rides, about 45 minutes in line for each.

That's **six hours standing in line** and **about forty minutes on rides**.

Half your day goes to waiting.

*Footnote: an illustrative estimate assuming 8 rides, a ~45-minute average standby and a ~5-minute average ride.*

### Beat 3 — The headliner
**Visual:** one oversized wait-time sign.
**On scroll:** the number ticks up from `0` to `61`, flashes `91` (the peak), and settles back on `61`.

**Copy**
Disneyland's Rise of the Resistance averages a **61-minute** wait. On a busy afternoon it peaks above **90 minutes**.

### Beat 4 — What guests think
**Visual:** two pictogram grids of 10 people. The US grid lights up 5 of 10; the UK grid lights up 7 of 10.

**Copy**
Ask guests what the worst part of a theme park is. Half of Americans and 70% of Brits give the same answer: **the lines**.

## Chapter 1 — The fix already exists

### Beat 5 — Free, then paid
**Visual:** a horizontal timeline that scrolls sideways as the reader scrolls down. Free systems are drawn in **green** and paid ones in **gold**.
**On scroll:** each item drops onto the timeline in turn. By the end, the green has nearly all turned to gold.

| | |
|---|---|
| 1999 | **FastPass**: free paper tickets for every guest |
| 2013 | **FastPass+**: free, booked from your phone |
| 2017 | **MaxPass**: Disneyland starts charging |
| 2017 | **Volcano Bay** opens with no physical lines |
| 2021 | **Genie+ / Lightning Lane** replace free FastPass |
| 2025 | **Volcano Bay** ends its virtual lines |
| 2026 | **Tokyo Disney** ends its free Priority Pass |

**Copy**
Parks solved this decades ago. Disney handed out free FastPasses in 1999, and a water park in Orlando opened with no physical lines at all.

Then, one by one, the free versions disappeared.

### Beat 6 — Why
**Visual:** one huge number on a dark background.
**On scroll:** the number counts up quickly.

**Copy**
# $724,000,000
That's what paid line-skipping made at Walt Disney World alone, in under three years. The figure comes from a leaked internal spreadsheet.

### Beat 7 — The price of skipping
**Visual:** a vertical price ladder that rises as you scroll: `$25` → `$42` → `$449`.

**Copy**
Today a Multi Pass costs $25 to $42 a day. The top tier, Premier Pass, reaches **$449 per person** on top of a park ticket.

The technology works. Parks have chosen to sell it as a way out of a line they built.

## Chapter 2 — The idea

### Beat 8 — A line doesn't make room
**Visual:** a ride drawn as a gate that admits a fixed number of dot guests per minute.
- **Scene A:** dots pile up into a long snake before the gate.
- **Scene B:** the same dots are spread across a park map, doing other things. Each one glides to the gate right on time.
- **On scroll:** a throughput counter stays the same in both scenes: `1,500 riders / hour`.

**Copy**
A queue doesn't make a ride hold more people. It only decides who rides, based on who's willing to stand there longest.

A schedule can make the same decision. The ride stays just as full, and nobody has to stand anywhere.

### Beat 9 — The receipt doesn't change
**Visual:** two park receipts side by side, labeled `Today` and `Proposed`. Their line items and totals match exactly.

| Today | Proposed |
|---|---|
| Park ticket · $100 | Park ticket (includes 8 ride reservations) · $100 |
| Fast pass × 4 · $80 | Extra ride reservation × 4 · $80 |
| **Total · $180** | **Total · $180** |
| Rides · 12 | Rides · 12 |
| Time in line · **~6 h** | Time in line · **0 h** |

**On scroll:** the receipts print line by line, in sync. The totals match, and then match again. The last line differs: today's `~6 h` is struck through and stamped `0 h`.

**Copy**
Here's the proposal, and the business model doesn't change at all.

Today a park sells you a $100 ticket, then four $20 fast passes during the day. The fast passes squeeze in four extra rides, but you still stand in line for the other eight.

Instead, sell the same $100 ticket with reservations included, and the same four $20 add-ons as extra ride reservations. You can still buy them on impulse from your phone, mid-afternoon.

The price is the same. The upsell is the same. The revenue and the number of rides are the same. The six hours in line are gone.

### Beat 9b — Everyone wins, not just the buyers
**Visual:** the same receipts, now for a guest who buys no add-ons: `$100 · 8 rides`. Today's version shows `~6 h` in line; the proposed version shows `0 h`.

**Copy**
And the guest who never buys an add-on? Today they get the worst day in the park. Under this model they get the same eight rides, and they don't wait for any of them.

### Beat 10 — How it works
**Visual:** a phone mockup that steps through four screens as you scroll:
1. **Pick:** tap the rides you want; order doesn't matter.
2. **Plan:** the app builds your day around meals and shows.
3. **Go:** a notification reads *"Space Mountain is ready for you in 10 min."*
4. **Adapt:** a ride breaks down, and your slot moves to later automatically.

**Copy**
Tell the park what you want to ride. It works out when.

If a ride breaks down or your lunch runs long, the plan adjusts. Each ride keeps a short buffer line so it always runs full. Pre-shows stay wherever they make the ride better.

## Chapter 3 — Dollars per enjoyment

### Beat 11 — A better metric
**Visual:** the Cost/Enjoyment chart appears, with cost on the X axis and enjoyment on the Y axis.
**On scroll:** points drop in one at a time:
- **Ride:** high cost, high enjoyment (top right).
- **Themed queue:** high cost, *below zero* enjoyment. It sinks under the axis.
- **Lawn:** almost free, decent enjoyment (top left).
- **Arcade or play area:** low cost, high enjoyment (top left).

**Copy**
Judge everything a park builds by one number: **dollars per enjoyment**. How much did it cost, and how much do guests actually enjoy it?

Rides score well. Lawns score brilliantly.

Queues are the worst-value thing in the park: expensive to build, and miserable to be in.

### Beat 12 — The most expensive waiting room
**Visual:** a sideways-scrolling walk through a castle queue: greenhouse → portrait gallery → headmaster's office → classroom → common room. Every room is richly drawn.
**On scroll:** the walk plays twice.
- **Pass 1, "Long line":** the rooms are packed with dots and a clock ticks in the corner. The details are hidden behind the crowd.
- **Pass 2, "Short line":** the rooms blur past at speed, and an attendant icon waves the reader along.
- **Pass 3:** the crowd is gone and the pace is slow. A label reads `Walk-through · Explore at your own pace`.

**Copy**
Universal's Harry Potter and the Forbidden Journey has one of the most beautiful queues ever built: a walk through Hogwarts castle, past moving portraits, Dumbledore's office and the Gryffindor common room.

Guests only ever see it in one of two ways.

When the line is long, it's a crowded waiting room.

When the line is short, you rush through, and staff wave you along past all the details.

Now picture the same castle as a walk-through you *choose* to visit, at your own pace. That's an attraction in its own right, and nobody has to queue to see it.

### Beat 13 — A guest in line can't buy a churro
**Visual:** a churro cart with a long queue next to it. The queue dissolves and dot guests flow over to the cart.

**Copy**
"But parks *need* lines, so people pay to skip them."

Do they? **75%** of US theme park guests say they sometimes skip food because of lines, and **65%** skip merchandise.

A guest standing in a queue can't spend anything. Give them their time back and they'll spend it in the park.

## Chapter 4 — Let them rest

### Beat 14 — Nowhere to sit
**Visual:** a split screen. On the left, metal queue rails and a lean bar. On the right, a shaded lawn with people lying in the grass.
**On scroll:** the right half widens until it fills the screen.

**Copy**
Queues are standing room only, and parks make sure there's nowhere comfortable to lie down. Even outside the lines, seating is scarce.

Physical discomfort is one of the top reasons guests leave a park unhappy, and benches and shade are what they ask for most.

A guest napping on a lawn costs the park nothing. On the dollars-per-enjoyment chart, rest beats everything else.

## Chapter 5 — What will everyone do instead?

### Beat 15 — The Day Bar, revisited
**Visual:** the same Day Bar from Beat 2.
**On scroll:** each gray block cracks open and fills with color: rides, wand-casting, an arcade, lunch, a lawn nap, a walk-through, another ride.

**Copy**
Take away six hours of standing in line, and you've given every guest six hours back.

The question isn't how to keep them busy. It's what's worth building for them.

### Beat 16 — It already works
**Visual:** a vertical stack of four cards that pins and advances one card per scroll step. Each card has a photo or illustration and one stat.

1. **Interactive wands**
   Guests roam the Wizarding World hunting for spots where a flick of a wand sets off an effect. Universal keeps expanding it, and Epic Universe added 14 more spots.
2. **Sorcerers of the Magic Kingdom**
   This was a free card game included with admission. Guests could play for 15 minutes between rides, or for 4 hours straight.
3. **Meow Wolf**
   A walk-through art experience with no ride system at all. Guests stay for a median of two hours. The Las Vegas location drew a million visitors in its first year.
4. **Pirate's Lair and Redwood Creek**
   These are Disneyland's own rope bridges, caves and climbing trails. Kids spend hours there, and nobody has to queue.

**Copy**
None of this is new. Parks already know how to build places where guests happily spend hours. There just aren't enough of them.

### Beat 17 — What to build
**Visual:** a scattered grid of icons that settle into place: bowling, an arcade, axe throwing, a jungle gym, a walk-through, mini golf, a splash pad, a lawn.

**Copy**
Bowling. Arcades. Axe throwing. Climbing structures. Mini golf. Walk-throughs. Lawns.

They cost far less than a ride, they have no hard capacity limit, and you can theme them as lavishly as anything else in the park.

### Beat 18 — Waiting that doesn't feel like waiting
**Visual:** two clocks side by side. One is labeled *empty time* and its hand drags. The other is labeled *busy time* and its hand races.

**Copy**
"Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time." That's David Maister's first law of waiting lines, from 1985.

Time with a known end feels shorter too. A reservation gives you both: something to do, and a moment when it ends.

### Beat 19 — Play right up to the ride
**Visual:** a cutaway of a ride building, with a themed play zone on the first floor and the ride loading area beyond it.
**On scroll:** a phone buzzes, and a dot guest leaves the play zone and walks straight onto the ride.

**Copy**
Better still, build the play zone into the ride building itself. You're halfway through a game when your phone says you're up, and you walk thirty seconds to the ride.

## Chapter 6 — Cruise ships figured this out

### Beat 20 — The ship
**Visual:** a cruise ship drawn in cross-section.
**On scroll:** a handful of headline experiences (theater, waterslides, surf simulator) glow in gold. Then dozens of other spaces light up in blue: pools, loungers, bars, trivia, mini golf, arcade, sports court.

**Copy**
A modern cruise ship carries thousands of guests and a few headline experiences. You reserve a show from the app, and the empty seats go to standby.

Everything else is open to everyone. Pools, loungers, bars, games: nobody waits in line for a pool.

### Beat 21 — Not perfect
**Visual:** a small aside card in a muted style.

**Copy**
Cruises aren't line-free. The waterslides on the biggest ships can still mean a 45-minute wait, and a seven-day sailing spreads demand across a whole week.

But the ratio is the lesson: a few headline experiences, and plenty to do everywhere else.

## Chapter 7 — "But what about…"

**Format:** a stack of flip cards. The front of each card shows the objection and the back shows the answer. On desktop, scrolling flips them; on touch, tapping does.

### Card 1 — "There isn't room on the big rides for everyone."
**Visual:** a bar chart. One bar is `48,800 guests in the park`; next to it, `21,000 seats on its most popular coaster`.
**Front:** Magic Kingdom's most popular coaster can carry fewer than half the park's guests in a day.
**Back:** True, and a queue doesn't fix that either. Today the line makes the cut: some guests wait an hour, and others see the sign and give up. Reservations make the same cut without anyone losing an hour. They can do it fairly, too, and the money saved on queues can build more capacity.

### Card 2 — "Disney tried free reservations. People hated them."
**Front:** FastPass+ meant booking rides 60 days ahead, and guests said it killed spontaneity.
**Back:** That was a planning race months in advance. Build it for the same day instead: tell the park what you want when you arrive, and let the plan adjust as the day goes.

### Card 3 — "A park tried no lines and gave up."
**Front:** Volcano Bay dropped its virtual lines in 2025.
**Back:** Its virtual waits still ran for hours, because there wasn't enough else to do. A virtual line with nothing to fill the time just moves the wait somewhere else. Universal also gave no reason for ending it, and paid Express passes became more attractive once it was gone.

### Card 4 — "Arcades flop."
**Front:** DisneyQuest closed after its attendance fell.
**Back:** DisneyQuest was a separately ticketed building whose games went more than a decade without an update. Play built into the park, to fill the time between rides, is a different idea.

### Card 5 — "Line-skipping is too profitable to give up."
**Front:** Paid skips earn hundreds of millions of dollars a year.
**Back:** Then keep it. Sell the same $20 add-on, but as an extra ride instead of an escape from the line. Same price, same impulse purchase, same revenue, and a better day for everyone.

## Production notes

- **Imagery:** use original illustrations, not park photos or film artwork. This avoids licensing problems with Disney and Universal and keeps the look consistent. Describe real places with real names, but draw them in your own style; the castle in Beat 12 should suggest Hogwarts without copying it.
- **Motion:** respect `prefers-reduced-motion`. With motion off, every beat should still work as a static graphic beside its text.
- **Mobile:** stack each graphic above its text. Show horizontal scrolls (Beats 5 and 12) as vertical steps.
- **Data honesty:** the 6-hour Day Bar is an estimate. Keep its footnote visible on the graphic itself, not only in the sources list.
- **Length:** about 1,500 words of reader-facing copy, which is roughly 6–8 minutes of scrolling.
